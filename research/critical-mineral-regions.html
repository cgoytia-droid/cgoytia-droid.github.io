Critical Mineral Regions:
From Mining Boom to Urban Value

Land Markets, Land-Based Financing and the Planning of Mining Corridors

The recent literature (2023–2026) marks an important departure from the classic view of “mining towns.” The focus is no longer only on how to house population growth, but on how to convert an extractive boom into long-term urban and territorial development. This note synthesizes the emerging consensus across eight themes, summarizes recent empirical findings and research gaps, and sketches a research agenda at the crossroads of urban economics, land-based financing and territorial development — with cases drawn from Argentina, Chile and Peru.

Eight areas of emerging consensus

1

The enclave remains the central risk

Many copper, lithium and critical-mineral projects generate enclave economies:

  • weak integration with the local economy;
  • specialized labor arriving from other regions;
  • few productive linkages;
  • cities dependent on a single price cycle.

The central question is no longer whether urban growth will occur, but which institutions allow an extractive enclave to become a diversified city. Outcomes depend far more on local governance than on the mineral itself.

2

A new paradigm: “resource urbanism”

An emerging field studies how critical minerals reorganize entire urban systems. Mining is analyzed not as an isolated project but as part of logistics corridors, energy infrastructure, ports, roads, intermediate cities and global supply chains.

In the Lithium Triangle, the notion of corridor urbanism is proposed: investment produces economic corridors connecting salt flats, ports and cities rather than new stand-alone mining towns.

Jujuy · Salta · Catamarca · N. Chile · S. Peru
3

Planning shifted from the city to the territory

The unit of planning is no longer only the mining city. The literature now proposes planning the functional region: networks of cities, corridors, water basins and regional infrastructure.

This reflects that workers, suppliers and services locate across several cities at once:

mine → service city → airport → port → regional capital.

4

Water is the primary urban constraint

In lithium especially, water is the main limit to development. Urban planning must incorporate:

  • water availability and water rights;
  • reuse infrastructure;
  • aquifer protection;
  • environmental monitoring.

The social license depends as much on water management as on the jobs created.

5

Successful mining cities are the ones that diversify

The goal should not be to build a “mining city,” but a city able to survive once the boom ends. Strategies use the boom to create permanent assets:

  • universities and technology centers;
  • regional hospitals and industrial parks;
  • renewable energy, tourism, the knowledge economy.
6

Renewed interest in intermediate cities

The main beneficiaries are often not the cities next to the mine, but intermediate cities of 50,000–500,000 inhabitants that concentrate specialized services, housing, education, logistics and commerce.

In Chile, a growing literature on Antofagasta, Calama and others asks how to avoid disorderly, mining-dependent growth.

7

Urban rent capture becomes a central theme

Part of the mining rent is capitalized into urban land, housing, industrial parks and infrastructure — yet few local governments capture that increment. Proposals now include:

  • land value capture; betterment levies;
  • capital-gains / plusvalía taxes;
  • local sovereign and territorial stabilization funds;
  • a strengthened property tax.

This field is still underdeveloped: integrated models of land value capture in mining regions are almost absent.

8

Resilience to the mining cycle

A very recent theme designs cities able to withstand falling copper and lithium prices, mine closure and automation. Researchers speak of adaptive mining cities, resilient mining regions and post-extractive urbanism.

The aim is to avoid infrastructure left oversized once the extractive cycle ends.

Argentina: the boom in numbers (PDAC 2026)

The structural paradox. In 2024, gold and silver still made up 82% of mining exports (gold alone 68%); the 2025 record was largely a gold-price effect over declining output. Lithium, meanwhile, collapsed from about USD 80,000/t (2022) to USD 10,000–11,000 (2024) — a vivid reminder that a city sized at the price peak is oversized in the trough. Copper and lithium are framed less as growth than as diversification away from that volatility.

A territorial map, not a single mining town

Under Article 124 of the Constitution the provinces own their subsoil, so each runs its own "mining paradiplomacy" — reinforcing the corridor, rather than single-city, reading of the boom:

San Juan — copper capital: Vicúña, El Pachón, Los Azules.
Catamarca — lithium + copper; the Agua Rica–Alumbrera corridor (MARA), 10,000+ construction jobs.
Salta — the lithium epicentre and "gateway to the Triangle."
Jujuy — Cauchari-Olaroz, the largest lithium operation (34,100 t in 2025).
Mendoza — a services city for the central Andean corridor (engineering, logistics, universities) without mining its own subsoil.
Patagonia — uranium, rare earths, vanadium and SMR/nuclear potential (Neuquén, Río Negro, Chubut, Santa Cruz).

Mendoza is the clearest illustration of the intermediate-city argument (theme 6): the main beneficiary of a mining cycle is often a services city in the network, not the settlement next to the deposit. What this investment map does not resolve — who captures the land-value uplift, how corridors are governed, how temporary workers are housed — is precisely the research gap below.

Recent empirical findings

  • Mining booms do increase urban population — but by less than traditionally assumed.
  • New employment concentrates mainly in services and commerce, rather than manufacturing.
  • Institutional quality explains much of the difference between successful and failed mining regions.
  • Early regional planning significantly reduces informal settlement formation and infrastructure deficits.

Important research gaps

From an urban-economics and planning perspective, studies are still missing that integrate:

  1. land markets in mining regions;
  2. value-capture mechanisms;
  3. urban financing associated with critical minerals;
  4. affordable housing for temporary workers;
  5. metropolitan governance of mining corridors;
  6. distributional effects between producing and non-producing municipalities;
  7. climate adaptation in extractive cities.

A research opportunity

There is a clear niche for an agenda combining urban economics, land-based financing and territorial development — a crossing between the literatures on land markets, land-based financing, resource urbanism and intermediate cities that remains scarcely developed internationally.
